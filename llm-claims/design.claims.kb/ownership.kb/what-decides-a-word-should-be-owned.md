# A word is owned where use signals violation

A word should be owned only where another theory's saying it is a
reliable sign of violation -- of encapsulation, of separation of
concerns, of the owning theory's primacy. Three goals set the test,
in order:

- keep theories orthogonal;
- stop agents and users smearing ideas between theories by accident;
- avoid false positives -- a license that cries wolf is worse than
  no license, because it trains its readers to wave findings through.

So the question to ask of a candidate word is counterfactual and
distributional: if this word turned up in some other theory, would
that be evidence of a concerns violation? A word plenty of unrelated
theories will need fails by construction -- use and violation are
uncorrelated there, so the license polices noise, and PLAIN's menu
applies: remove the ownership, or contract the entry to the term
genuinely coined.

The test mechanizes as concentration across theories, not rarity in
English. A word whose occurrences pile up in one theory makes an
occurrence elsewhere anomalous, which is the counterfactual exactly;
a word spread evenly across the fleet is ambient vocabulary whatever
its corpus frequency. Rarity answers a different question -- can a
reader supply the meaning unaided -- and the two disagree on real
cases: 'cost' is everyday English and still concentrated enough to
own, if it is.

The declined test is centrality: how much the coining theory invests
in a word says nothing about who else will need it. 'ontology',
'retraction', and 'transport' were each load-bearing for their
theories and still plain English -- ruled so 2026-08-20, overturning
an agent sort that had read centrality as coinage.
